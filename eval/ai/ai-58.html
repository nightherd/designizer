Before you spend anything

What this is, and what it isn’t.

It is

  • A daily lesson of 6–9 minutes of video, then about 12 minutes of you playing.
  • Reading music properly. Slowly, but properly. No coloured-letter shortcuts.
  • A short recording you send each Friday, with written feedback by Monday.
  • Fine on a £150 keyboard with 61 keys. Weighted keys help, but they can wait.

It isn’t

  • “Play any song in 30 days.” You’ll play nice things by week six. Chopin is a year two problem.
  • A gamified app with streaks. Missing a day is normal; we just pick up again.
  • For children. The pacing, the pieces and the jokes are for people over about 16.
  • Exam prep. If you want Grade 3 by June, a local teacher will serve you better.
Contents

Four parts, thirty-six weeks.

It’s laid out like the method books I learned from, because that structure works: a small idea, a short study to practise it, then a real piece that uses it.

Every piece is in the public domain or written for the course, so you get the sheet music as a printable PDF.

Questions

Things people ask before joining.

I’m 58. Is it too late?

No. Our oldest current student is 81. Adults learn more slowly in the hands and more quickly in the head. You’ll understand theory faster than a ten-year-old, and your fingers will catch up.

What do I need to buy?

A keyboard with at least 61 full-size keys and a sustain pedal input, and something to prop your phone on for recordings. We keep a short, unsponsored list of keyboards that work well, starting around £150.

I had lessons as a child and forgot everything.

Very common. Start at Part I anyway; you’ll move through the first few weeks quickly, and it’s worth fixing the habits you picked up at eight.

What if I miss a week?

Lessons stay unlocked, so you just carry on where you left off. If you fall far behind the term, you can roll into the next one at no cost.

Can I do it without reading music?

Not really. Reading is about a third of the course, and it’s what lets you keep learning after you finish. We go slowly enough that people who were sure they’d hate it usually don’t.
