Terra Incognita Five thousand years of drawing the world

Every map is an argument about what is known and what is not. This exhibition traces how people measured, guessed at, and redrew the planet, from clay tablets in Mesopotamia to the satellite surveys that now correct our coastlines by the metre.

Maps are never neutral. They are how a culture decides what matters.

A Babylonian clay disc from about 600 BCE placed its world inside a ring of ocean. Ptolemy gave Europe a grid of coordinates around the year 150. Medieval sailors drew coasts in ink that could be read by touch on rough seas. Each of these charts was right about some things and wrong about others, and most of the wrong parts were the ones nobody had yet gone to see.

The exhibition follows that uneven progress in four rooms and more than two hundred objects, including manuscripts, printed atlases, navigators' instruments, and digital surveys. Visitors can compare the same stretch of coast across six centuries and see where each mapmaker stopped looking.

Four rooms, read in order

Room one

The world as a disc

c. 3000 BCE to 500 CE

Early maps were tools for land records, temple plans, and cosmology. Clay, papyrus, and stone show the first attempts to fix distance and direction, and the first assumption that the edge of the known world is an ocean.

Room two

Coordinates and copies

c. 150 CE to 1450

Ptolemy's Geography introduced latitude and longitude, and later scribes copied it for centuries with errors that shifted whole continents. This room holds the manuscripts and the arguments they set off.

Room three

Sea charts and the edge of the page

c. 1270 to 1600

Portolan charts were built from compass bearings and sailing experience, not projections. Their coastlines are remarkably accurate, and their interiors are often blank. The room asks what a navigator needed to know, and what they were content to leave out.

Room four

Measured earth

c. 1700 to today

Triangulation, the chronometer, aerial survey, and satellite positioning turned mapping into measurement. The final room shows the last blank areas on the globe and how they were filled in, often by people who had lived on that land for generations.

Three objects to look for

Each is on display for the full run. Bring a pencil for the rubbings table in Room two.

Ring of the known world

Clay tablet, Mesopotamia, c. 600 BCE

A circular world surrounded by water, with the known lands in the middle. The inscription is nearly as old as the map.

Grid from the Geography

Manuscript copy, Constantinople, 15th century

The coordinate system that made it possible to say exactly where a place was, and to disagree about it in numbers.

Rhumb lines on vellum

Portolan chart, Majorca, c. 1375

A sailor's chart: coasts from bearings, and a web of compass lines to steer by. No projection, only experience.
